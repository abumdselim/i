{{I:compass}} The system comes first

Architecture is a business decision.

A system is a set of agreements: what a customer is, what an order means, which record wins when two disagree, who is allowed to reverse a decision. Those agreements outlive every framework we might choose. When they are wrong, no amount of clean code rescues the product.

So we ask uncomfortable questions early. What happens when the payment provider is unavailable for an hour? What does the warehouse team do when the stock count is wrong? Who reviews an automated decision, and what do they see on screen? A build that skips these questions does not avoid them. It postpones them to a worse moment, usually the week after launch.

The output of that work is short and unglamorous: a written architecture, a scope, a list of risks and a number. You can take it to another team, or to your board, without asking our permission.

What we look for before we start

  • {{I:users}}A problem that someone in the business owns, with the authority to change it.

{{I:target}} Fit

Where a model helps, and where it gets in the way.

We are specific about this because most disappointing projects come from applying the technology to a problem it cannot hold: no sample data, no way to score the output, no reviewer in the loop.

Worth building

{{I:upload}}Reading and routing documents
Invoices, claims, applications. High volume, consistent format, a person checking exceptions.
{{I:search}}Search across your own material
Contracts, policies, support history. The answer has to cite the passage it came from.
{{I:pen}}Drafting a first version
Proposals, replies, summaries. A person edits, and nothing leaves the building without review.
{{I:help}}First-line support
Repetitive questions with known answers, and escalation rules that stay explicit.

Better solved another way

{{I:shield}}Decisions with money or safety attached
Pricing exceptions, credit, clinical or legal judgement. A person decides, and signs their name.
{{I:gauge}}Problems nobody can score
If the team cannot describe a good answer, there is no way to know whether the system works.
{{I:workflow}}Anything that must repeat exactly
Deterministic rules belong in code, where they can be tested and audited line by line.
{{I:key}}Data you would not show a new hire
Permission problems are not solved by a prompt, and access is not a feature we can add later.

{{I:layers}} Three layers

Designed together, or not at all.

A working system needs all three. Most projects build one and hope the others follow.

01

Model

Chosen against a sample of your real work, not a demo dataset. Evaluated on the cases your team already knows are hard, and replaced without a rewrite when something better arrives.

  • Model selection and cost modelling
  • Evaluation set with known answers
  • Fallback for outages and refusals
02

Workflow

Decides what happens next: queue, retry, review, escalate. Every automated path has an owner, a record, and a switch that turns it off without taking the system down.

  • Review queues and escalation
  • Audit trail per decision
  • Kill switch and rollback
03

Interface

Shows the work and its provenance. Sources, confidence and edit history are visible, and the screen never pretends to know more than the system does.

  • Citations and confidence cues
  • Editable output, kept as a draft
  • Empty and failure states written first

{{I:flag}} Rules

Five rules we apply to every build.

These are working constraints, not values on a wall. They decide what gets built, what gets cut and when we say no.

{{I:approval}}R/01

No decision without a reviewer

If a person cannot inspect an automated decision and reverse it, that decision does not go live. The reviewer sees the input, the reasoning and the record.

{{I:gauge}}R/02

Evaluation before launch

A set of cases with known correct answers, written with your team, run on every change. If the score drops, the release stops.

{{I:database}}R/03

Data before models

If the source records disagree, the first work is reconciliation. A better model over inconsistent data produces confident nonsense.

{{I:sync}}R/04

A fallback that has run

A path that has never been exercised in production is not a fallback. We test the failure case on purpose, during working hours.

{{I:coin}}R/05

Cost is a design constraint

Token cost and latency decide which approaches are viable. We model both before choosing, so the system is still affordable at ten times the volume.
